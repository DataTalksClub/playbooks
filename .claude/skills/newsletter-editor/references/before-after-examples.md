# Before/After Newsletter Examples

Use these examples when a task needs concrete calibration beyond the style guide. The final files are text-only copies with embedded image payloads removed.

| Pair | Draft | Final | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working from a Phone | `examples/working-from-phone-draft.md` | `examples/working-from-phone-final-text.md` | The draft has too many small sections, too much scaffolding, or needs compression into a tighter personal workflow story. |

## Patterns visible across the examples

- Add a concrete opening before the machinery. The final versions usually start by grounding the system in a real constraint: travel, repeated student questions, or a new way of working with agent teams.
- Add a preview list when the post is long. The final list uses varied, natural bullets rather than rigid parallel phrasing.
- Group small tool sections under a larger workflow section when they are part of one setup. `Working from a Phone` turns many separate tool headings into `The remote setup` with focused subsections.
- Expand when the reader needs context. `FAQ System` grows because the final explains the course scale, Slack context, Google Docs limitations, bot architecture, website migration, and human review loop.
- Compress when the draft repeats operational detail. `Working from a Phone` removes dotfiles details, repeated setup explanations, source footnotes, and extra examples that do not move the story.
- Establish the method before the cases. `Building Projects with Agent Teams` first explains the agent roles, pipeline, batching, and tracking, then moves into the five projects.
- Use numbered case sections when a post surveys several experiments. This keeps repeated examples scannable without making every project feel like a separate article.
- Move from "what it is" to "why it became necessary". The final `FAQ System` does not start with the website URL. It starts with repeated student questions and the limits of Slack support.
- Keep a small amount of meta when it helps the reader navigate. The final versions use "In this post, I'll..." or "In this post, I'll show..." but avoid long article-about-itself paragraphs.
- Convert source footnotes and internal notes into normal prose, links, or cuts. The final articles do not keep generated source lists.
- Keep the concrete signal. The final versions preserve numbers such as 41 of 46 tasks completed, 5,000 to 25,000 students, 1,300 FAQ entries, 30 banner variants, and 5 minutes.
- Turn abstract claims into a sequence. The final `FAQ System` ending shows: Google Docs made contribution easy, Slack bot made content usable, website made it maintainable, automation made contributions practical, human review kept control.
- Keep one main example per section. When alternatives appear, they are usually shortened or moved aside so the section does not become a survey.
- Preserve Alexey's practical trade-offs. The finals keep "good enough", "I paused this", "I do not have time", "I learned the hard way", and other blunt qualifiers.
- Leave room for collaboration. The final `Working from a Phone` restores Valeriia's role in the writing process and a standalone thank-you.

## Editing passes learned from the examples

### First pass: structure

Fix the article shape before sentence polish. The strongest before/after differences are heading structure, opening, ending, and what gets merged or cut.

Common moves:

- Replace a generic title with a more specific one when needed.
- Remove draft frontmatter from reader-facing copy.
- Add or rewrite the preview list.
- Merge tool-by-tool sections into a workflow section.
- Split a long technical system into chronological stages.
- Move broad conceptual lessons after the concrete implementation.

### Second pass: narrative

Make every section follow a small story:

1. What was the situation?
2. What stopped working or became annoying?
3. What did Alexey try or build?
4. What changed after that?

The final versions rarely leave a tool as "here is a tool". They attach it to a constraint.

### Third pass: line edit

After the structure works:

- Fuse choppy adjacent sentences.
- Cut topic labels such as "The flow is simple" when the next sentence shows the flow.
- Replace formal lists with prose unless the list carries real structure.
- Keep concrete nouns and numbers.
- Use direct phrases like "too slow", "painful", "not enough", "good enough", and "I learned the hard way".

### Fourth pass: critique

Read the edit as if it were a new draft. Look for:

- The opening still starts too abstractly.
- A section has no result signal.
- An example is interesting but belongs in another article.
- A sentence was made smoother but less Alexey.
- A technical claim was added by the editor.
- The ending summarizes instead of landing on a practical change.

## How to use the pairs

When editing a new draft:

1. Pick the closest pair by structure, not by topic.
2. Compare headings first. Notice what was merged, split, promoted, or removed.
3. Compare openings and endings. These often changed more than individual sentences.
4. Then compare one representative middle section for sentence-level moves.
5. If the draft lacks key facts, propose the likely final structure and ask focused questions before rewriting.
6. Apply the pattern, not the exact wording.

Do not load all examples by default. Load only the pair that matches the current editing problem.
